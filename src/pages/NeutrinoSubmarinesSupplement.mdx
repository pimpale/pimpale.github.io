## Appendix A: The Weak Force

The weak force is mediated by the $W^+$, $W^-$, and $Z$ bosons. The $W^+$ and $W^-$ bosons mediate the [**charged current**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charged_current) interaction, and the $Z$ the [**neutral current**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutral_current) interaction. Charged current interactions are responsible for most of the radioactive decay implications of the weak force (as well as some scattering), and neutral current interactions are responsible for the rest of the elastic scattering events.

The weak force differs from the well known electromagnetic force in three ways:

1. Its [carrier particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_carrier) have mass. By the uncertainty principle, carrier particles with mass can only "exist" for a relatively short time before having to be reabsorbed, limiting how far they can travel, and thus the range of the force. In contrast, the carrier particle for electromagnetism, the photon, is massless, so it can travel arbitrarily far, meaning that its force is long ranged.
2. It is a [**chiral**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chirality_(physics)) theory. This means that the value of the quantum mechanical spin of a particle matters for whether it can undergo some interactions. Interactions can also flip the spin of a particle. There is no electromagnetic analogue to this behavior. See [here](https://www.quantumdiaries.org/2011/06/19/helicity-chirality-mass-and-the-higgs/) for a high quality explanation of chirality and helicity.
3. The charged-current interaction specifically can change the identity of particles, resulting in radioactive decay.

### Charged Current Interactions

The charged current interaction can result in the change of particle identity, particularly protons to neutrons and vice versa. It also can change the flavor of leptons (eg, convert a muon to an electron). It's the only interaction that can result in the creation or destruction of a neutrino.

Any charged current interaction must follow a few conservation laws:

1. [**Conservation of mass-energy**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_energy): This is a fundamental principle, but it's important here because some interaction routes involve creating heavier particles (like muons) from lighter ones (like electrons). For those interactions, the kinetic energy of the incoming particles must cover the energy budget needed to create that heavier particle.
2. [**Conservation of lepton flavor**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lepton_number#Lepton_flavor_conservation): We observe interactions like $\mu^- \to e^- + \bar\nu_e + \nu_\mu$: a muon decaying to an electron, an electron antineutrino, and a muon neutrino. Notice how an electron was created with an electron antineutrino, and how the muon left behind a muon neutrino. This points to the principle that the total number of electron-flavored particles minus electron flavored antiparticles must remain conserved over any interaction. The same rule applies for the muon and tau flavors.

Accounting for these, there are a few neutrino related interactions in total. We'll list all of them here, and note down their utility. We omit interactions with the [tau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tau_(particle)), since it is too high energy to appear commonly.

Within each group, we'll split them into the relatively broad categories of neutrino-producing and neutrino-detecting.

#### Nucleon interactions:

* Neutrino-producing:
  * $n \to p + e^- + \bar\nu_e$ (β⁻ decay)
    * very common decay mode in reactors. Produces the neutrinos we're interested in detecting
  * $p \to n + e^+ + \nu_e$ (β⁺ decay)
  * $p + e^- \to n + \nu_e$ (electron capture)
    * The two above reactions are rare decay modes in reactors
* Neutrino-detecting (transmutation)
  * $\bar\nu_e + p \to n + e^+$ (inverse beta decay)
    * the standard reaction used in reactor neutrino detectors, but note that only 30% of reactor neutrinos (calculated from data in [Perrisse et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.14992v2)) have the energy needed to access this interaction
  * $\nu_e + n \to p + e^-$
    * can't be used for antineutrino detection, but can be useful in astronomy (eg, detecting supernova neutrinos)
  * $\nu_\mu + n \to p + \mu^-$
  * $\bar\nu_\mu + p \to n + \mu^+$
    * The above two reactions demonstrate that a sufficiently energetic muon neutrino can catalyze the production of a muon. Very rare though, and useless for us since we're interested in detecting 1 MeV neutrinos that are too low-energy.

#### Lepton interactions:

* Neutrino-producing
   * $\mu^- \to e^- + \bar\nu_e + \nu_\mu$ (muon decay)
   * $\mu^+ \to e^+ + \nu_e + \bar\nu_\mu$ (antimuon decay)
     * the above two interactions are not useful, but neatly illustrate lepton flavor conservation
* Neutrino-detecting (Scattering)
   * $\nu_\mu + e^- \to \mu^- + \nu_e$
   * $\bar\nu_e + e^- \to \bar\nu_\mu + \mu^-$
     * These reactions involve a neutrino scattering off an electron, and converting it to a muon in the process. Reactor neutrinos are too low-energy to access this channel.
   * $\nu_e + e^- \to \nu_e + e^-$
   * $\bar\nu_e + e^- \to \bar\nu_e + e^-$
     * The above two interactions describe charged current elastic scattering. Note that neutral currents can also result in elastic scattering off of electrons, but the charged current version tends to have a higher rate of interactions. It requires the neutrino and lepton to be of the same flavor, though.

### Neutral Current Interactions

The neutral current, mediated by the [Z boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W_and_Z_bosons#Z_bosons), can't change the flavor of any particle, but can result in the exchange of spin, momentum, and energy between neutrinos and other particles.

There are two ways the neutral current can interact with a particle: a **vector interaction**, and an **axial interaction**. The charged current also displays both vector and axial behavior, but it's less relevant for understanding its interactions.

#### Vector Interaction

The vector interaction of the neutral current works much like the electric field that we are used to. In this interaction, the Z boson is as a weak interaction analogue of the photon. Just like the exchange of a virtual photon results in attraction or repulsion between two electromagnetically charged particles, an exchange of a virtual Z boson results in attraction or repulsion between two [weak charged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_charge) particles, although the effect is hardly visible outside of a nearly head on collision (due to the mass of the Z boson).

Here is a table of weak charges for neutrinos, nucleons, and leptons:

| Particle | Approximate vector weak charge ($Q_W$) |
|---|---:|
| Neutrinos: $\nu_e, \nu_\mu, \nu_\tau$ | $+1$ |
| Charged leptons: $e^-, \mu^-, \tau^-$ | $\approx -0.05$ |
| Proton: $p$ | $\approx +0.07$ |
| Neutron: $n$ | $\approx -0.99$ |

The neutron and neutrinos interact strongly, but the interaction strength between protons and charged leptons is relatively weak.

However, there is another channel for neutrino-proton and neutrino-electron interactions, the axial interaction, which we'll discuss in the next section.

Before we move on, we'll note that neutral current interactions between a neutrino and a nucleus containing many neutrons can occur *coherently*. This means that the wavefunction amplitudes of this interaction happening add together constructively, and result in a much higher cross section than otherwise expected. This phenomenon is known as [**coherent elastic neutrino-nucleus scattering (CEvNS)**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherent_elastic_neutrino-nucleus_scattering), and has been [observed](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12310522) in experiments.

#### Axial Interaction

The axial interaction applies to all fermions in the Standard Model - that is, quarks, leptons, and neutrinos. Like the vector interaction, it is flavor-neutral, any neutrino may participate.

Every particle that can participate in axial interactions has a coupling factor ($g_A$ in literature) that varies from particle to particle, and measures how much it participates.

So far, this is just like the vector weak charge ($Q_W$), but there's a difference: the overall strength of the interaction depends not only on this coupling factor, but also on the relative alignment of the spins of the incoming and target particle.

Whether it's better for the interacting particles' spins to be aligned or anti-aligned depends on the exact particle combination, but it can change the rate of interaction dramatically.

One note about axial interactions is that they have a chance to flip the spin of the particle they interact with. We'll call this a **neutral-current axial spin transition**. Unfortunately, when dealing with whole nuclei, we don't benefit from coherency, since nucleons in a nucleus pair their spins up. Only the last nucleon added has a spin that the neutrino can interact with.

#### Interactions

We'll list all of the reactor-neutrino-relevant interactions from the neutral current here. To keep things concise, we'll focus on just nucleons and electrons as targets. We'll also list all both neutrinos and antineutrinos, of all flavors, as just $\nu$, since the neutral current affects all of them.

#### Nucleon interactions:

* $\nu + p \to \nu + p$
* $\nu + n \to \nu + n$
  * Neutrino-neutron interactions provide most of the cross section here, as $|Q_W|$ is greater for neutrons than it is for protons.

#### Lepton interactions:

* $\nu + e^- \to \nu + e^-$
  * If $\nu$ is an electron neutrino, the charged-current elastic channel dominates the total rate.
* $\nu + e^-_{\uparrow} \to \nu + e^-_{\downarrow}$ (neutral-current axial spin transition on the electron)
  * The axial interaction can flip the electron's spin.

#### Whole Nucleus interactions

* $\nu + A \to \nu + A$ (CEvNS)
  * This has a high cross section since the vector interaction's amplitude adds coherently accross the entire nucleus.
* $\nu + A_{\uparrow} \to \nu + A_{\downarrow}$ (neutral-current axial spin transition on the nucleus)
  * Only available for nuclei with unpaired nucleons.

## Appendix B: Number of Antineutrinos Produced by a Nuclear Submarine

We want to find the number of antineutrinos produced by an SSBN when operating in patrol mode.

### Find Steady State Power Consumption

We define $P_{SS}$ as the steady state electrical and mechanical power consumption of a nuclear submarine operating in patrol mode. The actual fission power produced will be higher, since the reactor is not perfectly efficient.

$$P_{SS} = P_{hotel} + P_{propulsion}$$

$P_{hotel}$ is the **hotel load**: the electrical power required to maintain standard life support systems. This load doesn't change no matter how fast the submarine is going. $P_{propulsion}$ is the power consumed for propulsion purposes. This scales superlinearly with regards to speed.

#### Estimating Hotel Load

The hotel load for Ohio-class SSBNs is not public. However, we can make an educated first principle guess based on other submarines where we have better data.

[Comparative Naval Architecture of Modern Foreign Submarines](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA202251.pdf), a thesis written in 1980, cites the hotel load of the [Kilo-class submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilo-class_submarine) at 131.32 kW, and the [Type 2400](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upholder/Victoria-class_submarine) at 115.94 kW.

The Kilo-class submarine supports a crew of 52, and the Type 2400 a crew of 59. Both of these are diesel-electric attack submarines. In general, hotel load is critical for diesel-electric submarines, since it directly relates to how long a submarine is able to dwell underwater.

In contrast, the nuclear-powered Ohio class submarine is under much less tight power constraints, and also has a crew around 3x the size, at 155. My guess is that it uses more than 3x the power, due to said relaxation of constraints. Additionally, the amount of electronics has gone up dramatically since the 1980s.

Thus, we'll estimate $P_{hotel}$ at 500 kW. This is just an educated guess, and I wouldn't be surprised if it wasn't super accurate. I'm relatively confident it's the right order of magnitude though.

#### Estimating Propulsion Power Draw

When not loitering, the largest source of power consumption is the power consumed to propel the submarine through water. The submarine must overcome drag and other dynamic effects.

To estimate $P_{propulsion}$ We utilize the formula described in [Ragheb (2025)](https://mragheb.com/NPRE%20402%20ME%20405%20Nuclear%20Power%20Engineering/Nuclear%20Marine%20Propulsion.pdf):

$$
P_{mobility}=\frac{1}{2}\rho\frac{\pi D^2}{4}V^3
$$

where:

* $\rho$ is the density of seawater, ~1025 kg/m³
* $D$ is the diameter of the submarine, ~12.8 m (the Ohio class has a 42 ft beam)
* $V$ is the velocity of the submarine, unknown

$V$ will vary based on what the submarine is doing. In general, SSBNs have a high max speed (in order to enable evading torpedos), but generally reduce their speed to a minimum when patrolling. The patrolling speed of the Ohio-class submarines is not public.

A review of open source literature indicates the likely speed is around [Miasnikov (1990)](https://www.armscontrol.ru/subs/snf/snf03221.htm) estimates that the "maximum low noise" speed is around 8 knots (4 m/s). Wikipedia's article on [tactical speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tactical_speed) (drawn from [Polmar & Moore (2004)](https://archive.org/details/coldwarsubmarine0000polm)) cites the max speed at which the passive sonar system can detect other ships at 8-13 knots.

All of this points to a speed under 8 knots. Speed is still desirable though, to avoid being pinned down. Altogether, I'd estimate 6 knots (~3 m/s) as a reasonable guess for $V$.

Plugging in these values:

$$
P_{mobility}=\frac{1}{2}(1025)\frac{\pi (12.8)^2}{4}(3)^3 \approx 1.8 \text{ MW}
$$

### Total Power

We can now sum together the two sub-estimates to get the total useful power generation:

$$P_{SS} = 2.3 \text{ MW}$$

The reactor is not totally efficient at converting fission energy into usable energy. There are necessarily losses. Nuclear power plants [operate](https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/nuclear-power-reactors/overview/nuclear-power-reactors) between 33-37% efficiency.

However, I'd suspect that the nuclear reactor onboard a submarine is on the less efficient side. Nuclear power plants have efficiencies of scale from using larger reactors, and no restrictions on weight or sound. They can afford to use machinery impractical onboard a submarine. I'd estimate 30% efficiency ($a_{eff}$) is a reasonable guess.

Thus, the total thermal power can be found as:

$$
P_{thermal} = \frac{P_{SS}}{a_{eff}} = \frac{2.3}{0.3} = 7.667 \text{ MW}
$$

We can cross check this by using an estimate from a [recently declassified CIA report](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/DOC_0005512850.pdf) which cites "In large nuclear submarines, the rate of coolant flow can be several thousand gallons per minute, with an increase in temperature of 10 C or more".

It's vague exactly what the coolant flow is, but we'll treat it as 5,000 gal/min. With an increase of temperature of 10 °C, we can recover the thermal power using the heat equation:

$$
P_{thermal} = \dot{m} \, c \, \Delta T
$$

where:

* $\dot{m}$ is the mass flow rate of the coolant
* $c$ is the specific heat of water, ~4186 J/(kg·K)
* $\Delta T$ is the temperature rise, 10 °C

Converting the flow rate, 5000 gal/min $\approx$ 315.4 kg/s (treating the coolant as 1 kg/L). Plugging in:

$$
P_{thermal} = (315.4)(4186)(10) \approx 13.2 \text{ MW}
$$

This is roughly 1.7x our 7.67 MW estimate, but the same order of magnitude, which is reassuring given the coarseness of both estimates.

### Counting Antineutrinos

We'll use our conservative estimate of about 7.667 MW for the remainder of the calculations.

In order to find out the rate of antineutrinos produced by a nuclear submarine, we must first find the rate of fission events, as antineutrino production is directly proportional. The thermal power produced by the reactor is the direct product of the rate of fission events and the energy produced by one fission (and subsequent decays):

$$
P_{thermal} = E_{fission} \times R_{fission}
$$

Submarine nuclear reactors use highly enriched uranium to maximize power output per volume, and extend the working life of the reactor. Thus, they can be modeled as burning almost exclusively U-235.

$E_{fission}$ for U-235 is well characterized. [Kopeikin et al (2004)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/hep-ph/0505081) estimate it as 201.7 MeV ($3.231 \times 10^{-11}$ J)

Thus:

$$
R_{fission}=\frac{P_{thermal}}{E_{fission}}=\frac{7.667 \times 10^{6} \text{ J/s}}{3.231 \times 10^{-11} \text{ J}} \approx 2.37 \times 10^{17} \text{ fissions/s}
$$

Since each U-235 fission yields 6.08 antineutrinos:

$$
R_{\bar\nu}=6.08 \times R_{fission} \approx 1.44 \times 10^{18} \text{ } \bar\nu/\text{s}
$$

## Appendix C: IBD Cross Section of Proton

The inverse beta decay cross section of the proton has been described in depth by [Vogel & Beacom (1999)](https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-ph/9903554). We use their lowest order estimation (Equation 11 in the paper), as it has the simplest formula, and is relatively accurate at low energies:

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{IBD}}(E_\nu) \approx 0.0952 \left( \frac{E_e   p_e}{1 \text{ MeV}^2} \right) \times 10^{-42} \ \text{cm}^2
$$

where:

* $E_e = E_\nu - \Delta$ is the positron's total energy, with $\Delta = m_n - m_p \approx 1.293$ MeV. ($m_n$ and $m_p$ refer to the mass of the neutron and proton, respectively).
* $p_e = \sqrt{E_e^2 - m_e^2}$ is the positron's momentum, with $m_e$ (the mass of the positron) $\approx 0.511$ MeV
* all energies and momenta are in MeV

The most important feature of the cross section is the **kinematic threshold**. The reaction can only proceed when there is enough energy to convert the proton to the neutron and then produce the positron at rest.

$$
E_\nu \geq \left(m_n -m_p\right) + m_e \approx 1.806 \ \text{MeV}
$$

Below this energy, the cross section is zero (by conservation of energy). Above it, it grows quadratically with $E_\nu$.

### Spectrum-Averaged Cross Section

The 1.806 MeV threshold is a serious limitation for reactor antineutrino detection. The U-235 antineutrino spectrum peaks well below 1 MeV.

To get the actual interaction rate, we need to know more about the distribution of antineutrinos produced in a nuclear reactor (the second plot in the figure below). [Perrisse et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.14992v2) provide this data. We use it to define the probability density function (PDF) for neutrino energy $p(E)$.

We can now weight the cross section by the probability that a neutrino is actually emitted by the reactor at that frequency. Integrating $\sigma_{\mathrm{IBD}}(E) \cdot p(E)$ over energy gives the spectrum-averaged cross section per proton. This product is shown in the third plot in the figure.

When we do the math, we find that the resulting effective per-proton cross section is approximately:

$$
\langle \sigma_{\mathrm{IBD}} \rangle \approx 1.04 \times 10^{-43} \ \text{cm}^2 \ \text{per proton}
$$

This is the number used in the main text to estimate detection rates.

## Appendix D: IBD + ES Detector Media

The kind of medium we want depends on which channel we are optimizing for, as well as the platform constraints that we're working under. Luckily, the two channels have largely overlapping criteria.

For **IBD**, only free protons can participate in the reaction. There are analogs to IBD for heavier nuclei, but unless the isotopes are carefully selected, all other non-hydrogen atoms (such as carbon and oxygen) are effectively dead weight. Thus, the ideal IBD medium simply maximizes the density of hydrogen atoms.

For **electron scattering (ES)**, every electron in the medium can participate, no matter which element it belongs to or which orbital it's in. Naively, it seems like the best electron scattering media should prioritize heavier elements, since heavier elements have more electrons per atom. However, heavier elements also tend to include more neutrons. While every proton in the nucleus contributes a corresponding electron, neutrons in the nucleus do not contribute an electron, and are effectively dead weight. The ideal electron scattering medium is thus hydrogen as well. All stable isotopes other than hydrogen have at least 1 neutron per proton, making them half as electron-dense.

This works out well for us, since hydrogen-rich media can optimize both channels relatively well. The main divergence in criteria between IBD media and electron scattering media is how much non-hydrogen elements are penalized. For IBD, any non-hydrogen elements are penalized quite harshly, with 0% the value of hydrogen, whereas for ES, any relatively light nuclei have a penalty of only about 50% the value of hydrogen.

In general, we care more about hydrogen atoms per unit mass (gravimetric density) rather than per unit volume (volumetric density). This is mostly a function of the platforms we want to mount the neutrino detector on being mobile. Satellites, airplanes, and cargo ships have relatively strict weight limits. A stationary detector might prioritize something else.

However, we do need the volumetric density to be fairly reasonable, ruling out gases, and the remarkably undense liquid hydrogen.

We'll list the common media and both their hydrogen and electron densities (in both volumetric and gravimetric terms):

* **Water**
  * **Volumetric H Density:** $6.7\times10^{22}/\text{ cm}^3$
  * **Gravimetric H Density:** $6.7\times10^{25}/\text{ kg}$
  * **Volumetric e⁻ Density:** $3.3\times10^{23}/\text{ cm}^3$
  * **Gravimetric e⁻ Density:** $3.3\times10^{26}/\text{ kg}$
  * Cheap, safe, and transparent. Pure water doesn't scintillate, so water-based designs must either rely on Cherenkov light from the positron (as in [Super-Kamiokande](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super-Kamiokande)), or dissolve a small fraction of organic scintillator into the water. This approach, **water-based liquid scintillator (WbLS)**, is relatively new. It's used in [ANNIE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accelerator_Neutrino_Neutron_Interaction_Experiment) and proposed for THEIA.
  * Water is the only scintillator variant that also offers Cherenkov-based directional information.
* **[Linear alkylbenzene (LAB)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_alkylbenzene)**
  * **Volumetric H Density:** $6.3\times10^{22}/\text{ cm}^3$
  * **Gravimetric H Density:** $7.3\times10^{25}/\text{ kg}$
  * **Volumetric e⁻ Density:** $2.9\times10^{23}/\text{ cm}^3$
  * **Gravimetric e⁻ Density:** $3.4\times10^{26}/\text{ kg}$
  * Transparent, chemically stable, and safe to handle. Recent designs use it more frequently due to its superior scintillation properties compared to the mineral oil/pseudocumene blend used earlier. Used in [Daya Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daya_Bay_Reactor_Neutrino_Experiment), [RENO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reactor_Experiment_for_Neutrino_Oscillation), and the upcoming [JUNO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiangmen_Underground_Neutrino_Observatory).
* **[Pseudocumene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1,2,4-Trimethylbenzene)**
  * **Volumetric H Density:** $5.3\times10^{22}/\text{ cm}^3$
  * **Gravimetric H Density:** $6.0\times10^{25}/\text{ kg}$
  * **Volumetric e⁻ Density:** $2.9\times10^{23}/\text{ cm}^3$
  * **Gravimetric e⁻ Density:** $3.3\times10^{26}/\text{ kg}$
  * More toxic and flammable than LAB, but excellent scintillation yield and very high transparency, which is critical for very large monolithic detectors. Used in [KamLAND](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KamLAND) and [Borexino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borexino), blended with other materials.
* **Mineral oil / paraffin**
  * **Volumetric H Density:** $7.3\times10^{22}/\text{ cm}^3$
  * **Gravimetric H Density:** $8.6\times10^{25}/\text{ kg}$
  * **Volumetric e⁻ Density:** $2.9\times10^{23}/\text{ cm}^3$
  * **Gravimetric e⁻ Density:** $3.4\times10^{26}/\text{ kg}$
  * Inexpensive, and among the densest in hydrogen of common media on both axes. Doesn't scintillate on its own, so needs to be doped (5% for [NOvA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1504.04035), 20% for [KamLAND](https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/0404071)) with a scintillator like pseudocumene. However, has poor scintillation characteristics, necessitating more sensitive phototubes.
* **Plastic scintillator ([Polystyrene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polystyrene) or [Polyvinyl Toluene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyvinyl_toluene))**
  * **Volumetric H Density:** $5.2\times10^{22}/\text{ cm}^3$
  * **Gravimetric H Density:** $5.1\times10^{25}/\text{ kg}$
  * **Volumetric e⁻ Density:** $3.3\times10^{23}/\text{ cm}^3$
  * **Gravimetric e⁻ Density:** $3.3\times10^{26}/\text{ kg}$
  * A solid organic scintillator. Can be machined into segmented cells, each of which independently tags the position of the interaction. Used in [CHANDLER](https://cnp.phys.vt.edu/chandler/).

However, if we are willing to stray into exotic materials, there exist more hydrogen-dense options. These exist on a Pareto frontier of gravimetric density and volumetric density, where the more gravimetrically dense options tend to have low volumetric density and vice versa:

We'll look at a few more exotic options:

* **Lithium Borohydride**
  * **Volumetric H Density:** $7.4\times10^{22}/\text{ cm}^3$
  * **Gravimetric H Density:** $1.1\times10^{26}/\text{ kg}$
  * **Volumetric e⁻ Density:** $2.2\times10^{23}/\text{ cm}^3$
  * **Gravimetric e⁻ Density:** $3.3\times10^{26}/\text{ kg}$
  * [Lithium Borohydride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium_borohydride) is one of the [most hydrogen-dense materials known](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Volumetric-and-gravimetric-hydrogen-density-of-some-selected-hydrides-Mg2FeH6-shows-the_fig7_248555402), with a hydrogen mass fraction of around 18.5%. However, like most boranes, it is relatively expensive, and reacts violently with both water and air, impeding its usage in practice. For military applications where weight is paramount (satellites or aircraft), these downsides could likely be overcome.
* **Densified Liquid Methane**
  * **Volumetric H Density:** $6.8\times10^{22}/\text{ cm}^3$
  * **Gravimetric H Density:** $1.5\times10^{26}/\text{ kg}$
  * **Volumetric e⁻ Density:** $1.7\times10^{23}/\text{ cm}^3$
  * **Gravimetric e⁻ Density:** $3.8\times10^{26}/\text{ kg}$
  * [Liquid methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquefied_natural_gas) (CH₄), with a hydrogen mass fraction of around 25%, has the highest gravimetric H density of any simple, chemically inert material. SpaceX stores it deeply subcooled (near its freezing point at ~91 K, density ~0.45 g/cm³) as fuel for the [Raptor engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Raptor), so the aerospace industry has already worked out how to handle it at scale. Methane doesn't scintillate, so it would need to be doped or use Cherenkov radiation.

Notice that all of these media have volumetric and gravimetric densities of hydrogen and electrons that are relatively similar to each other. There are no order-of-magnitude differences. Thus, the choice between these media is driven less by raw H or electron content and more by other factors like transparency (for large detectors), pulse shape (for background rejection), toxicity, and compatibility with neutron-capture dopants.

## Appendix E: Electron-Neutrino Scattering Cross Sections

[Fundamentals of Neutrino Physics and Astrophysics](http://theor.jinr.ru/~vnaumov/Eng/JINR_Lectures/books/Giunti2007.pdf) (Ch 5) has a derivation of the antineutrino cross sections for the various flavors. They provide a table for the scattering in the high energy limit (when $\sqrt{s} \gg m_e$):

| Process | Total cross-section (in units of $10^{-46}\ \text{cm}^2$) |
|---|---|
| $\nu_e + e^-$ | $\dfrac{G_F^2 s}{4\pi}\left[(1 + 2\sin^2\vartheta_W)^2 + \tfrac{4}{3}\sin^4\vartheta_W\right] \approx 93\, s/\text{MeV}^2$ |
| $\bar\nu_e + e^-$ | $\dfrac{G_F^2 s}{4\pi}\left[\tfrac{1}{3}(1 + 2\sin^2\vartheta_W)^2 + 4\sin^4\vartheta_W\right] \approx 39\, s/\text{MeV}^2$ |
| $\nu_{\mu,\tau} + e^-$ | $\dfrac{G_F^2 s}{4\pi}\left[(1 - 2\sin^2\vartheta_W)^2 + \tfrac{4}{3}\sin^4\vartheta_W\right] \approx 15\, s/\text{MeV}^2$ |
| $\bar\nu_{\mu,\tau} + e^-$ | $\dfrac{G_F^2 s}{4\pi}\left[\tfrac{1}{3}(1 - 2\sin^2\vartheta_W)^2 + 4\sin^4\vartheta_W\right] \approx 13\, s/\text{MeV}^2$ |

Here, $s$ is the total energy of the two incoming particles squared (a [Mandelstam variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandelstam_variables)). For our case of a moving neutrino striking an electron effectively at rest,

$$s \approx 2 m_e E_\nu.$$

Using this, we can convert this formula into a formula more similar to the one given in [Appendix C](#appendix-c-ibd-cross-section-of-proton):

* For electron antineutrinos:
  * $\sigma_{\bar\nu_e e}(E_\nu) \approx 3.99 \cdot (E_\nu/\text{MeV}) \times 10^{-45}\ \text{cm}^2$

* For muon and tau antineutrinos:
  * $\sigma_{\bar\nu_{\mu,\tau} e}(E_\nu) \approx 1.34 \cdot (E_\nu/\text{MeV}) \times 10^{-45}\ \text{cm}^2$

However, for our purposes, what we care about is the reactor-spectrum weighted cross section. The following graph shows the cross section and spectrum as a function of antineutrino energy:

  alt="Electron Scattering"

When we integrate over the spectrum, we find:

* $\langle \sigma_{\bar\nu_e + e^-} \rangle \approx 5.79 \times 10^{-45}\ \text{cm}^2$ per electron
* $\langle \sigma_{\bar\nu_{\mu,\tau} + e^-} \rangle \approx 1.94 \times 10^{-45}\ \text{cm}^2$ per electron

Note that these are likely overestimates, especially at low energies, where we need to take into account that the electron will not end up with an ultrarelativistic energy. However, because the overall cross section is much lower than IBD's (1/18th the IBD cross section), this doesn't make a difference to the end result.

Another source of error (coinciding with the first) is that in practice, we cannot detect arbitrarily low-velocity electrons. The electrons need to be moving fast enough to scintillate or cause Cherenkov radiation.

### Comparison Against IBD in Pure Hydrogen

Hydrogen is the ideal medium for both channels, and each hydrogen atom contributes exactly one free proton (an IBD target) and one electron (an ES target). This makes pure hydrogen a convenient benchmark for comparing the two channels head to head. Using the spectrum-averaged per-target cross sections ([Appendix C](#appendix-c-ibd-cross-section-of-proton) for IBD):

| Channel | $\langle \sigma \rangle$ per hydrogen atom |
|---|---|
| IBD | $1.04 \times 10^{-43}\ \text{cm}^2$ |
| ES ($\bar\nu_e$) | $5.79 \times 10^{-45}\ \text{cm}^2$ |

Per hydrogen atom, IBD's cross section is about 18x larger than ES's. The gap narrows in real media, since every element contributes electrons but only hydrogen contributes free protons: LAB has ~4.7 electrons per hydrogen atom, reducing IBD's advantage to ~3.9x, and densified methane has ~2.5, for an advantage of ~7.2x. In every practical medium, IBD remains the dominant channel — and since ES also lacks IBD's double-flash background rejection, it is at best a secondary channel. This is why the main text considers IBD alone.

## Appendix F: IBD & Electron-Scattering Cross Sections

We want to compute the reactor-neutrino spectrum normalized cross section of two candidate media: densified liquid methane and linear alkylbenzene (LAB). For each of these, we'll compute both the IBD cross section (which scales with hydrogen content) and the electron-scattering (ES) cross section (which scales with total electron content), per kilogram and per cubic meter.

### Densified Methane

We'll assume the methane is stored deeply subcooled near its freezing point (~91 K), as in [Raptor engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Raptor) propellant tanks. At this temperature it has a density of roughly $0.45 \text{ g/cm}^3$. We'll also ignore any dopants or wavelength shifters that would be needed to make it scintillate or to capture the neutron. These additives typically make up a few percent of the total mass at most.

The properties of methane are:

* **Chemical formula:** $\mathrm{CH_4}$
* **Molar mass:** $M = 12.011 + 4 \times 1.008 \approx 16.04 \text{ g/mol}$
* **Density:** $\rho \approx 0.45 \text{ g/cm}^3 = 450 \text{ kg/m}^3$
* **Hydrogen atoms per molecule:** $n_H = 4$
* **Electrons per molecule:** $n_e = 6 + 4 = 10$
* **Hydrogen mass fraction:** $f_H = \dfrac{4 \times 1.008}{16.04} \approx 0.251$

Now, we can solve for hydrogen density, both per kg and per cubic meter:

* **H/kg:**

$$
\frac{N_H}{m} = \frac{n_H \cdot N_A}{M} = \frac{4 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}}{16.04 \text{ g/mol}} \approx 1.502 \times 10^{23} \text{ H/g} = 1.502 \times 10^{26} \text{ H/kg}
$$

* **H/m³:**

$$
\frac{N_H}{V} = \rho \cdot \frac{N_H}{m} = (450 \text{ kg/m}^3)(1.502 \times 10^{26} \text{ H/kg}) \approx 6.76 \times 10^{28} \text{ H/m}^3
$$

And the corresponding electron densities:

* **e/kg:** $\dfrac{N_e}{m} = \dfrac{n_e \cdot N_A}{M} = \dfrac{10 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}}{16.04 \text{ g/mol}} \approx 3.76 \times 10^{26} \text{ e/kg}$
* **e/m³:** $\dfrac{N_e}{V} = \rho \cdot \dfrac{N_e}{m} \approx 1.69 \times 10^{29} \text{ e/m}^3$

Using the results from [Appendix C](#appendix-c-ibd-cross-section-of-proton) (IBD) and [Appendix E](#appendix-e-electron-neutrino-scattering-cross-sections) (ES, using the no-oscillation $\bar\nu_e$ value $5.79 \times 10^{-45}\ \text{cm}^2/\text{e}$), the gravimetric and volumetric cross sections are:

* **Per kg:**

$$
\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{IBD}}}{m} = \frac{N_H}{m} \cdot \langle \sigma_{\mathrm{IBD}} \rangle = (1.502 \times 10^{26} \text{ H/kg})(1.04 \times 10^{-43} \text{ cm}^2/\text{H}) \approx 1.56 \times 10^{-17} \text{ cm}^2/\text{kg}
$$

$$
\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{ES}}}{m} = \frac{N_e}{m} \cdot \langle \sigma_{\bar\nu_e e} \rangle \approx (3.76 \times 10^{26})(5.79 \times 10^{-45}) \approx 2.18 \times 10^{-18} \text{ cm}^2/\text{kg}
$$

* **Per m³:**

$$
\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{IBD}}}{V} = \frac{N_H}{V} \cdot \langle \sigma_{\mathrm{IBD}} \rangle = (6.76 \times 10^{28} \text{ H/m}^3)(1.04 \times 10^{-43} \text{ cm}^2/\text{H}) \approx 7.03 \times 10^{-15} \text{ cm}^2/\text{m}^3
$$

$$
\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{ES}}}{V} = \frac{N_e}{V} \cdot \langle \sigma_{\bar\nu_e e} \rangle \approx (1.69 \times 10^{29})(5.79 \times 10^{-45}) \approx 9.79 \times 10^{-16} \text{ cm}^2/\text{m}^3
$$

### LAB

Linear Alkylbenzenes are a class of related compounds. We need to pick a specific one. We'll be using [dodecylbenzene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dodecylbenzene) as our stand-in for this class. However, the values for other LABs will be quite similar (likely within a 5% difference). As in the case of methane, we're going to ignore the other dopants added. Our values will be for pure dodecylbenzene, without considering the other fluors or additives. This won't change the results by more than a few percent at most.

The properties of dodecylbenzene are:

* **Chemical formula:** $\mathrm{C_{6}H_{5}\text{-}C_{12}H_{25}} = \mathrm{C_{18}H_{30}}$
* **Molar mass:** $M = 18 \times 12.011 + 30 \times 1.008 \approx 246.4 \text{ g/mol}$
* **Density:** $\rho \approx 0.86 \text{ g/cm}^3 = 860 \text{ kg/m}^3$
* **Hydrogen atoms per molecule:** $n_H = 30$
* **Electrons per molecule:** $n_e = 18 \times 6 + 30 = 138$
* **Hydrogen mass fraction:** $f_H = \dfrac{30 \times 1.008}{246.4} \approx 0.123$

Now, we can solve for hydrogen density, both per kg and per cubic meter:

* **H/kg:**

$$
\frac{N_H}{m} = \frac{n_H \cdot N_A}{M} = \frac{30 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}}{246.4 \text{ g/mol}} \approx 7.33 \times 10^{22} \text{ H/g} = 7.33 \times 10^{25} \text{ H/kg}
$$

* **H/m³:**

$$
\frac{N_H}{V} = \rho \cdot \frac{N_H}{m} = (860 \text{ kg/m}^3)(7.33 \times 10^{25} \text{ H/kg}) \approx 6.30 \times 10^{28} \text{ H/m}^3
$$

And the corresponding electron densities:

* **e/kg:** $\dfrac{N_e}{m} = \dfrac{n_e \cdot N_A}{M} = \dfrac{138 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}}{246.4 \text{ g/mol}} \approx 3.37 \times 10^{26} \text{ e/kg}$
* **e/m³:** $\dfrac{N_e}{V} = \rho \cdot \dfrac{N_e}{m} \approx 2.90 \times 10^{29} \text{ e/m}^3$

The gravimetric and volumetric cross sections are:

* **Per kg:**

$$
\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{IBD}}}{m} = \frac{N_H}{m} \cdot \langle \sigma_{\mathrm{IBD}} \rangle = (7.33 \times 10^{25} \text{ H/kg})(1.04 \times 10^{-43} \text{ cm}^2/\text{H}) \approx 7.62 \times 10^{-18} \text{ cm}^2/\text{kg}
$$

$$
\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{ES}}}{m} = \frac{N_e}{m} \cdot \langle \sigma_{\bar\nu_e e} \rangle \approx (3.37 \times 10^{26})(5.79 \times 10^{-45}) \approx 1.95 \times 10^{-18} \text{ cm}^2/\text{kg}
$$

* **Per m³:**

$$
\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{IBD}}}{V} = \frac{N_H}{V} \cdot \langle \sigma_{\mathrm{IBD}} \rangle = (6.30 \times 10^{28} \text{ H/m}^3)(1.04 \times 10^{-43} \text{ cm}^2/\text{H}) \approx 6.55 \times 10^{-15} \text{ cm}^2/\text{m}^3
$$

$$
\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{ES}}}{V} = \frac{N_e}{V} \cdot \langle \sigma_{\bar\nu_e e} \rangle \approx (2.90 \times 10^{29})(5.79 \times 10^{-45}) \approx 1.68 \times 10^{-15} \text{ cm}^2/\text{m}^3
$$

### Aircraft & Cargo Ship Cross Sections

To contextualize these numbers, we'll compute the total IBD cross section of a Boeing 747 freighter (using densified methane, since gravimetric density is what matters for an aircraft) and a supertanker (using LAB, which is the standard scintillator for large stationary detectors and has a density well-matched to crude oil tankers). For each platform, we have to consider both the **payload mass limit** and the **cargo volume limit**. Whichever constraint is more restrictive will set the achievable cross section.

#### Boeing 747-400F (densified methane)

The [747-400F freighter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_747-400#747-400F) has:

* **Max payload:** $\approx 113{,}000 \text{ kg}$
* **Cargo volume:** $\approx 710 \text{ m}^3$

The volume of densified methane that would saturate the payload limit is:

$$
V_{\text{payload}} = \frac{113{,}000 \text{ kg}}{450 \text{ kg/m}^3} \approx 251 \text{ m}^3
$$

This is well under the 710 m³ cargo volume, so **payload mass is the binding constraint**. The total cross section is:

$$
\sigma_{747}^{\mathrm{IBD}} = (113{,}000 \text{ kg}) \cdot (1.56 \times 10^{-17} \text{ cm}^2/\text{kg}) \approx 1.76 \times 10^{-12} \text{ cm}^2
$$

$$
\sigma_{747}^{\mathrm{ES}} = (113{,}000 \text{ kg}) \cdot (2.18 \times 10^{-18} \text{ cm}^2/\text{kg}) \approx 2.46 \times 10^{-13} \text{ cm}^2
$$

#### VLCC Supertanker (LAB)

We use a [Very Large Crude Carrier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_tanker#Size_categories) (VLCC) as the cargo ship reference. Since LAB is a liquid with density comparable to crude oil ($\sim 870\text{-}920 \text{ kg/m}^3$), a tanker is the natural platform. A typical VLCC has:

* **Deadweight tonnage (DWT):** $\approx 300{,}000 \text{ t} = 3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ kg}$
* **Cargo capacity:** $\approx 340{,}000 \text{ m}^3$

The mass of LAB that would fill the cargo volume is:

$$
m_{\text{vol}} = (340{,}000 \text{ m}^3)(860 \text{ kg/m}^3) \approx 2.92 \times 10^8 \text{ kg}
$$

This is just under the DWT limit, so **cargo volume is the binding constraint** (only marginally — the two constraints are nearly co-saturated, since LAB density is close to crude oil density, which is what the ship was designed for). The total cross section is:

$$
\sigma_{\text{VLCC}}^{\mathrm{IBD}} = (340{,}000 \text{ m}^3) \cdot (6.55 \times 10^{-15} \text{ cm}^2/\text{m}^3) \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-9} \text{ cm}^2
$$

$$
\sigma_{\text{VLCC}}^{\mathrm{ES}} = (340{,}000 \text{ m}^3) \cdot (1.68 \times 10^{-15} \text{ cm}^2/\text{m}^3) \approx 5.7 \times 10^{-10} \text{ cm}^2
$$

## Appendix G: Idealized Radiochemical Cross Section

The radiochemical antineutrino detection method effectively functions like inverse beta decay, but on heavy nuclei:

$$
\bar\nu_e + A(Z, N) \to A(Z-1, N+1) + e^+
$$

The key idea is that we can choose a pair of (Z,N) such that the product nucleus has more nuclear binding energy than the original nucleus. This generally means a proton rich isotope. Adding a neutron and removing a proton will make the nucleus more stable. The difference in nuclear binding energies "subsidizes" the cost of converting a proton into a neutron, and the weight of the positron.

We must be careful here, because if the target nucleus has too much nuclear binding energy, the original nucleus might spontaneously decay to that product nucleus through electron capture, where:

$$
A(Z, N) + e^- \to A(Z-1, N+1) + \nu_e
$$

An isotope is only stable against electron capture if the captured electron's rest mass is not enough to bridge the gap between the parent and daughter nuclear masses. The energy balance for EC is:

$$
M_\text{nuc}(Z,N)c^2 + m_e c^2 = M_\text{nuc}(Z-1,N+1)c^2 + E_\nu
$$

An isotope is marginally stable against EC when the neutrino carries away zero energy, i.e. when the daughter nucleus is heavier than the parent by exactly one electron mass:

$$
\Delta M_\text{nuc} \equiv M_\text{nuc}(Z-1,N+1) - M_\text{nuc}(Z,N) = m_e
$$

So, if we want to drive inverse beta decay on this marginally stable nucleus, the incoming neutrino needs to weigh $2m_e$. The first electron mass is to pay off $\Delta M_\text{nuc}$, which has a minimum value of $m_e$ in order for the isotope to be stable. The second $m_e$ is the mass of the positron that will be created. Altogether, this gives us a minimum bound of 1.022 MeV.

Note that radiochemical methods work *much* better for normal neutrino detection, where the minimum bound is exactly 0 MeV. This is because the desired reaction (producing a proton from a neutron) gives us energy that can subsidize the creation of the electron.

### Estimating Ideal Per-Nucleus Cross Section

In order to determine if radiochemical methods are worth investigating further, we'll compute a rough upper bound on the cross section. We'll assume we have an isotope that has exactly the minimum bound of 1.022 MeV.

Unfortunately, there aren't many good calculations of the cross section for isotopes other than hydrogen. However, we can try approximating it by just using the formula for hydrogen, but just replacing the proton kinematic bound of 1.806 MeV with the new one. This is most likely an optimistic estimate. At reactor neutrino energies, free-proton IBD is essentially a "best case". This is because the incoming antineutrino has only one channel to interact with the nucleus: flipping the proton to a neutron. In this channel, the beginning and end states have high "wavefunction overlap". They are very similar (consisting of a single nucleon). Thus, it has a high cross section.

However, this is not the case in a larger nucleus. First, note that in a large nucleus, neutrons and protons have a specific lowest-energy state that they sit in normally, but there are many other "excited" states. The daughter nucleus, with its different proton and neutron count, has its own ground state, which generally has a structure that doesn't closely resemble the parent's. These rearrangements lead to lower wavefunction overlap (and thus lower cross section). The state where almost all nucleons are in the same place, but one proton has turned into a neutron (an [Isobaric Analog State](https://inspirehep.net/files/4a269ff2f25283a98814b4237d69e627)) is almost always an excited state that will require supplying a lot of energy to reach, something most reactor neutrinos don't have.

So, using the same form as the free-proton cross section:

$$
\sigma(E_\nu) \approx 0.0952 \left( \frac{E_e \, p_e}{1 \text{ MeV}^2} \right) \times 10^{-42} \ \text{cm}^2
$$

where:

* $E_e = E_\nu - m_e$ is the positron's total energy. We've used the marginal-stability condition $\Delta M_\text{nuc} = m_e \approx 0.511$ MeV in place of the proton's $\Delta = m_n - m_p \approx 1.293$ MeV.
* $p_e = \sqrt{E_e^2 - m_e^2}$ is the positron's momentum, with $m_e$ (the mass of the positron) $\approx 0.511$ MeV.
* all energies and momenta are in MeV.

Integrating this against the U-235 antineutrino spectrum (using the same procedure as in [Appendix C](#appendix-c-ibd-cross-section-of-proton)) gives a spectrum-averaged per-nucleus cross section of:

$$
\langle \sigma \rangle \approx 2.1 \times 10^{-43} \ \text{cm}^2 \ \text{per target nucleus}
$$

## Appendix H: CEvNS Cross Sections

The CEvNS differential cross section was first written down by [Freedman (1974)](https://journals.aps.org/prd/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevD.9.1389) shortly after the discovery of weak neutral currents. Detailed nuclear-structure calculations of the form factor for specific isotopes are given by [Van Dessel et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03658), whose Equation 11 we use here:

$$
\frac{d\sigma}{dT}(E_\nu, T) = \frac{G_F^2 M}{4\pi} Q_W^2 \left(1 - \frac{T}{E_\nu} - \frac{M T}{2 E_\nu^2}\right) F^2(q^2)
$$

where:

* $T$ is the kinetic energy of the recoiling nucleus.
* $M$ is the nuclear mass.
* $E_\nu$ is the incoming neutrino energy.
* $G_F \approx 1.166 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{MeV}^{-2}$ is the Fermi coupling constant.
* $F(q^2)$ is the nuclear form factor, which accounts for the loss of coherence when the momentum transfer $q$ resolves the nucleus's internal structure. At reactor energies $qR \lesssim 0.1$, so $F^2(q^2) \approx 1$ to better than 1% and we drop it.
* $Q_W$ is the **weak nuclear charge**:

$$
Q_W = N - (1 - 4\sin^2\theta_W) Z \approx N
$$

with $\sin^2\theta_W \approx 0.231$. Because the coefficient $1 - 4\sin^2\theta_W \approx 0.04$ is small, the proton contribution is suppressed and $Q_W^2 \approx N^2$ to a few percent. This is the famous coherent enhancement: the cross section grows with the *square* of the neutron count, derived in the main text from summing scattering amplitudes in phase.

### Kinematics

For a neutrino of energy $E_\nu$ elastically scattering off a nucleus of mass $M$, the maximum recoil kinetic energy is fixed by two-body kinematics:

$$
T_\text{max} = \frac{2E_\nu^2}{M + 2E_\nu} \approx \frac{2E_\nu^2}{M}
$$

The approximation holds because $E_\nu \ll M$ always (reactor neutrinos are MeV-scale; even light target nuclei are tens of GeV). The recoil energy goes as $E_\nu^2$, not $E_\nu$ — the same kinematic suppression that makes a ping-pong ball bounce off a bowling ball without moving it.

This lets us simplify the differential cross section. The two kinematic correction terms behave very differently at $T \to T_\text{max}$:

* $M T / (2 E_\nu^2)$ reaches **1** at $T = T_\text{max}$, so it's an O(1) correction that controls the shape of the recoil spectrum.
* $T / E_\nu$ reaches only $2 E_\nu / M$ at $T = T_\text{max}$, which is $\sim 10^{-4}$ for reactor neutrinos on heavy nuclei.

So we drop the $T/E_\nu$ term and the form factor, leaving the simplified form we'll use for the rest of this appendix:

$$
\frac{d\sigma}{dT}(E_\nu, T) \approx \frac{G_F^2 M}{4\pi} Q_W^2 \left(1 - \frac{T}{T_\text{max}}\right)
$$

The differential cross section is then nearly flat in $T$ between 0 and $T_\text{max}$, with a linear falloff at the upper end.

### Integrating Above a Detector Threshold

Real detectors only see recoils above some minimum energy $T_\text{min}$. Integrating $d\sigma/dT$ from $T_\text{min}$ to $T_\text{max}$ gives the closed-form per-nucleus cross section seen by the detector:

$$
\sigma(E_\nu, T_\text{min}) = \frac{G_F^2 Q_W^2 E_\nu^2}{4\pi} \left(1 - \frac{M T_\text{min}}{2 E_\nu^2}\right)^2
$$

valid for $E_\nu > E_\nu^\text{thr} \equiv \sqrt{M T_\text{min}/2}$; below this kinematic cutoff the detector is blind because no allowed recoil reaches $T_\text{min}$.

Three pieces read off cleanly:

* **$N^2$ enhancement**: $\sigma \propto Q_W^2 \approx N^2$. Heavy nuclei win per scatterer.
* **Quadratic energy dependence**: at $T_\text{min} = 0$, we recover the textbook $\sigma_\text{tot}(E_\nu) \propto E_\nu^2$. Plugging in constants gives the often-quoted form

$$
\sigma_\text{tot}(E_\nu) \approx 4.2 \times 10^{-45} \, N^2 \left(\frac{E_\nu}{\text{MeV}}\right)^2 \ \text{cm}^2
$$

* **Threshold suppression**: the factor $(1 - T_\text{min}/T_\text{max})^2$ tells us how much of the per-nucleus rate the detector loses to its threshold. It enters quadratically right at the most populated part of the reactor flux, so it dominates the comparison between detector technologies.

### Kinematic Cutoffs by Detector

Below is the kinematic cutoff $E_\nu^\text{thr}$ for the four detector media discussed in the main text:

| Target | $N$ | Nuclear mass $M$ | $T_\text{min}$ (NR) | $E_\nu^\text{thr}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Si (CCD)               | 14  | 26 GeV  | ~50 eV  | 0.8 MeV |
| Ge (HPGe ionization)   | 40  | 68 GeV  | ~1 keV  | 5.8 MeV |
| Ge (cryo bolometer)    | 40  | 68 GeV  | ~50 eV  | 1.3 MeV |
| Kr (HP gas TPC)        | 48  | 78 GeV  | ~150 eV | 2.4 MeV |

The reactor antineutrino spectrum (see [Appendix C](#appendix-c-ibd-cross-section-of-proton)) peaks around 1 MeV and cuts off near 8 MeV. HPGe ionization has $E_\nu^\text{thr} = 5.8$ MeV — meaning the detector is *only* sensitive to the small fraction of the reactor spectrum in the highest-energy tail. A Ge bolometer of the same isotope, with a 20× lower threshold, sees essentially the entire spectrum and inherits the same $N^2 = 1600$ enhancement. A factor of 20 in threshold is worth far more than a factor of $40^2/14^2 \approx 8$ in $N^2$ — which is why per-target-mass, the cryogenic bolometer technologies are projected to dominate reactor CEvNS.

### Spectrum-Averaging

As in [Appendix C](#appendix-c-ibd-cross-section-of-proton), the actual interaction rate per target nucleus requires averaging the closed-form $\sigma(E_\nu, T_\text{min})$ against the [Perrisse et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.14992v2) U-235 antineutrino spectrum $p(E_\nu)$:

$$
\langle \sigma \rangle = \int_{E_\nu^\text{thr}}^{\infty} p(E_\nu) \, \sigma(E_\nu, T_\text{min}) \, dE_\nu
$$

For a per-mass comparison we further divide by the nuclear mass; the per-kg figure of merit is then proportional to $(N^2/A)\langle \sigma / \sigma_\text{tot} \rangle$, which makes the threshold penalty explicit as the fraction of the textbook cross section actually accessible.

The threshold cliffs are sharply visible in the top panel: each curve drops to zero below its $E_\nu^\text{thr}$. In the bottom panel — where the spectrum has already cut off the high-energy tail — the Ge cryogenic bolometer dominates because its threshold sits below the spectrum peak, while the Ge HPGe ionization channel sees essentially nothing despite the same $N^2$. Spectrum-averaging gives the per-kg figures of merit:

The Kr entry stands in for the noble-gas TPC class; [Appendix I](#appendix-i-choosing-the-noble-gas-for-a-gas-tpc) compares Ar, Kr, and Xe and explains why the choice of noble gas barely matters.

Two caveats apply when comparing these formulas to real measurements:

* **Quenching.** For ionization-readout detectors (HPGe, Si CCDs), only a fraction of $T$ becomes detectable ionization — typically 15-25% in Ge, parameterized by a Lindhard-style quenching factor. The $T_\text{min}$ in the table above is the *true nuclear recoil* threshold; the measured electron-equivalent threshold is correspondingly lower, and the quenching factor at sub-keV is the dominant systematic in current experiments. Bolometers and gas TPCs do not suffer this penalty.
* **Form factor.** At reactor energies the $F^2(q^2) \approx 1$ approximation is excellent. For stopped-pion sources ($E_\nu \sim 30$ MeV), the form factor begins to bite and must be included.

### Platform Cross Sections for a Cryogenic Phonon Bolometer

We can put the best CEvNS technology — a Ge cryogenic phonon bolometer, $\langle \sigma \rangle \approx 1.0 \times 10^{-16} \text{ cm}^2/\text{kg}$ — on the same platforms as the IBD media in [Appendix F](#appendix-f-ibd--electron-scattering-cross-sections). Germanium ($\rho \approx 5.32 \text{ g/cm}^3$) is far denser than any IBD liquid, so mass is the binding constraint on both platforms, and the total cross section is just the per-kg figure times the payload limit:

* **Boeing 747-400F** (113,000 kg payload): $\sigma \approx 1.13 \times 10^{-11} \text{ cm}^2$, about $6.4\times$ the same aircraft filled with densified methane.
* **VLCC supertanker** ($3.0 \times 10^8$ kg DWT): $\sigma \approx 3.0 \times 10^{-8} \text{ cm}^2$, about $14\times$ the same tanker filled with LAB.

The advantage is larger on the ship because germanium saturates the mass limit while LAB is volume-limited, so IBD pays its full dead-mass penalty there. These are idealized figures — cooling hundreds of tonnes of germanium to the millikelvin regime is well beyond current cryogenics — but they show CEvNS beating IBD platform-for-platform.

## Appendix I: Choosing the Noble Gas for a Gas TPC

A high-pressure gas TPC can run on any of the heavy noble gases — argon, krypton, or xenon. Naively the heaviest target should win: the CEvNS cross section scales as $N^2$, and Xe ($N = 78$) has over three times the neutron count of Ar ($N = 22$), an $N^2$ advantage of more than $12\times$. The plot below compares all three at a common 150 eV nuclear-recoil threshold, per kg of target.

The raw $N^2$ advantage almost entirely cancels. Spectrum-averaging gives:

* Xe HP gas TPC: $\langle \sigma \rangle \approx 5.0 \times 10^{-17} \ \text{cm}^2/\text{kg}$
* Ar HP gas TPC: $\langle \sigma \rangle \approx 4.3 \times 10^{-17} \ \text{cm}^2/\text{kg}$

## Appendix J: Literature Review of Speculative High-Sensitivity Techniques

Physicists have proposed many approaches to the problem of detecting neutrinos with high sensitivity. They are primarily interested in the problem of detecting "relic" neutrinos: neutrinos generated during the earliest stages of the universe's expansion. These neutrinos have an extremely low energy, and thus require extremely high sensitivity to be detected. But, many of the proposals originally intended for relic neutrino can be repurposed for submarine detection.

A brief literature review follows, focusing only on the approaches that could feasibly be adapted to reactor neutrinos:

* [Arvinataki et. al (2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04021): This paper introduces the concept of using **superradiant spin transitions** to detect relic neutrinos. The key observation is that neutrinos can interact with atoms by flipping their atomic spins. Because the exact identity of which atom was flipped is concealed, the atoms form a collective quantum state that has a much larger cross section than any individual atom. The effective cross section scales with $N^2$, and enables a 10 cm-sized sphere to gain a a ~$10^{21}$ boost over incoherent scattering.
  * Follow ups:
    * [del Castillo et. al (2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.20357): Different authors re-analyzed the nuclear-spin scheme with an open-quantum-system simulation that included experimental imperfections, concluding that the mechanism is real, but detection of relic neutrinos is still far out of reach using modern technology.
    * [Galanis et. al (2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.20520): The same authors of the original superradiant spin transition paper created an implementation protocol for the original proposal. Their experimental realization has worse sensitivity than their original proposal, but could be constructed with modern day technology.
* [Jones & Formaggio (2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.11765): Jones & Formaggio show that a Bose-Einstein condensate of the isotope ⁸³Rb could emit neutrinos superradiantly, accelerating its effective decay rate from 86 days to minutes in a ~$10^6$-atom condensate. They call this a "neutrino laser". However, they also discuss the prospect of running the process in reverse to create a "superabsorber". They note the idea faces difficulties due to the fact that the first few neutrino captures are not boosted superadiantly. However, this constraint can be worked around simply by making the Bose-Einstein condensate larger.

The above mechanisms appear the most feasible, but there are several others with non-zero applicability, listed here for completeness:

* [Bauer & Shergold (2021)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.12784): They propose using a particle accelrator to store nuclei in ion storage rings. These particles fly around the racetrack. Thus, their momentum relative to the neutrinos are increased. Because cross section is boosted with the square of momentum, this can boost the cross section immensely. The difficulty is that it requires a whole accelerator ring, which is impractical to keep on a mobile platform.

## Appendix K: Coherent Interactions

*This section explains how coherent interactions are possible, and what their fundamental limits are.*

The rest of the methods we'll be discussing leverage **coherent interaction** with the neutrino to boost cross section. This is a powerful method that allows increasing the cross section by orders of magnitude. In order to understand it, there are several fundamental quantum concepts that are prerequisites we'll need to cover first.

### Wave-Particle Duality

This is a classic result in quantum mechanics which states that all particles have both wavelike and particlelike properties depending on the context. An extremely rough rule of thumb is that in cases of high mass or energy, the wavelength is short, and the particle-like properties are emphasized, whereas in cases of low mass and energy, the wavelength is long, and the wavelike properties become prominent. Matter waves travel through space like waves, but when they interact, they are localized to a particular point.

The previous effects we discussed relied mostly on the particle-like nature of the neutrino, discussing interactions with particular nucleons or electrons. However, neutrinos do act like waves too, and this is what coherent effects harness.

In the real world, particles like the neutrino are **wavepackets**. When they're emitted from a nucleus, they have a certain uncertainty in momentum but a relatively definite position (that of the nucleus that just decayed). As they traverse space, that momentum uncertainty causes their position to spread out into a wavepacket-like shape: a plane wave under a localized envelope.

<Figure
  src={WavepacketImg}
  alt="Gaussian wavepacket"
/>

However, in our case, we'll consider the neutrino as a pure **plane wave**, where the momentum is definite but the position of the particle is completely undefined. This is mostly just for ease of conceptual understanding and calculation.

This plane wave's wavelength is the **[de Broglie wavelength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter_wave)** $\lambda = h/p$. For an ultrarelativistic particle, $E \approx pc$, so $\lambda \approx hc/E$. Plugging in $hc \approx 1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}$, a 1 MeV neutrino has

$$
\lambda_\nu \approx \frac{1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}}{10^6\ \text{eV}} \approx 1.24\ \text{pm} \quad (1.24 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{m})
$$

This is exactly the same wavelength a 1 MeV gamma ray would have, since the formula $\lambda = hc/E$ applies to both. The neutrino's tiny rest mass is negligible at MeV energies, so it behaves just like a photon for the purposes of wavelength.

To contextualize this length, it's a much smaller distance than the size of an atom ($\sim 0.1\ \text{nm}$), but much larger than the size of a single nucleon ($\sim 1\ \text{fm}$).

### Scattering Amplitudes

The second core concept that we need to understand is the scattering amplitude. Thus far, we discussed scattering as a probabilistic event that either happens or doesn't. This is true in terms of observed results. But, at the quantum scale, how this happens is more subtle.

As an example, observe the following animation, depicting a wavepacket impinging on a barrier:

<Figure
  src={WaveThickBarrierImg}
  alt="Wavepacket scattering off a thick barrier"
/>

The wall interacts with the wavefunction, splitting it into a portion that bounces back and a portion that continues through. Later, when the particle is detected, we will find that *most* of the time it scattered back, but a small fraction of the time it tunneled through. The case of a neutrino scattering off some target is the same, except that since neutrinos are so weakly interacting, the overwhelming majority of the wavefunction continues forward and only a tiny fraction scatters back:

<Figure
  src={WaveThinBarrierImg}
  alt="Wavepacket scattering off a thin barrier"
/>

The **scattering amplitude** represents how much how much of the wavefunction gets scattered. Note that it is a complex number, since it deals directly with the wavefunction. When we have many targets, the portions of the wavefunction that get scattered can add together coherently and produce a much larger amplitude than naively expected. It's only at the end, when we make an observation, that we square the amplitude to get a probability density.

### Coherent Scattering

In the two cases we'll be considering: many nucleons inside a nucleus, or many atoms inside a small region, there are multiple scatterers. We can't tell from the outside from which particle the neutrino scattered, even in principle. We can thus treat the entire scattering as a single quantum event, summing together the scattering amplitudes of of each potential scatterrer.

Note that this is a necessary, but not sufficient condition to receive coherent amplification, as we'll see soon.

Suppose we have $N$ identical scattering centers at positions $\vec x_1, \vec x_2, \dots, \vec x_N$. The incoming neutrino has momentum $\vec p_\text{in}$ (as a plane wave, $e^{i\vec p \cdot \vec x / \hbar}$), and the outgoing neutrino has momentum $\vec p_\text{out}$. The **momentum transfer** is $\vec q = \vec p_\text{in} - \vec p_\text{out}$. Each scattering center contributes the same per-center amplitude $f$, but with a position-dependent phase:

$$
\mathcal{A}_\text{total} = \sum_{j=1}^{N} f \, e^{i \vec q \cdot \vec x_j / \hbar}
$$

The total cross section is proportional to $|\mathcal{A}_\text{total}|^2$.

If $|\vec q| R / \hbar \gg 1$, then the scattering is **incoherent**. This is because the phases $e^{i \vec q \cdot \vec x_j / \hbar}$ are spread all around the unit circle. They average out, so $|\mathcal{A}_\text{total}|^2 \approx N |f|^2$. This means that the cross section grows linearly with respect to $N$. This is the default case with most neutrino scattering, and it's what we've utilized so far.

However, if $|\vec q| R / \hbar \ll 1$, the scattering will be **coherent**. This is because all the phases are roughly the same, allowing the amplitudes to add in phase. The cross section  $|\mathcal{A}_\text{total}|^2 \approx N^2 |f|^2$. The cross section will thus grow with the *square* of the number of scattering centers.

The extra factor of $N$ is the coherent enhancement effect. For a medium nucleus with $A \sim 100$ nucleons, it's a boost of roughly two orders of magnitude over incoherent per-nucleon scattering. For more speculative schemes which involve scattering across multiple atoms it can be much more.

#### Why Multi-Nucleus Scattering is Possible

The neutrino wavelength is larger than a nucleus, so CEvNS - coherent scattering from a nucleus - is easily permitted. However, it naively seems that scattering from more than one nucleus is impermissible, as the gap between atoms (typically around 0.1-0.3 nm) is far higher than the neutrino's de Broglie wavelength of 1.24 pm.

But this is actually not the case. Neutrinos *can* scatter coherently from multiple atoms, since it's **momentum transfer** that sets the phase of the scattering amplitude, not the [wavenumber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavenumber).

To see why, we'll break down the interaction. There is an incoming neutrino plane wave $e^{i \vec p_\text{in} \cdot \vec x / \hbar}$ and an outgoing neutrino plane wave $e^{i \vec p_\text{out} \cdot \vec x / \hbar}$. The phase relevant to coherent scattering off a center at position $\vec x_j$ is the difference of incoming and outgoing phases:

$$
e^{i \vec p_\text{in} \cdot \vec x_j / \hbar} \cdot e^{-i \vec p_\text{out} \cdot \vec x_j / \hbar} = e^{i (\vec p_\text{in} - \vec p_\text{out}) \cdot \vec x_j / \hbar} = e^{i \vec q \cdot \vec x_j / \hbar}
$$

So even though $|\vec p_\text{in}|$ and $|\vec p_\text{out}|$ are individually large, their difference, $\vec q$, can be small if the scattering angle is small.

And, indeed, we see that neutrons, which typically have wavelengths that are quite short owing to their large mass (and thus momentum) do in fact scatter coherently (a fact utilized in [neutron diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_diffraction)).

The key requirement is that the scattering angle be small. For elastic scattering at angle $\theta$ off a target much heavier than the neutrino's energy, the magnitude of $\vec q$ is approximately

$$
|\vec q| \approx 2 |\vec p_\text{in}| \sin(\theta/2)
$$

which can be much smaller than $|\vec p|$ itself. But, for 1 MeV neutrinos, the maximum angle possible that still maintains coherence between two atoms 0.2 nm apart is:

$$\theta_\text{max} \approx 10^{-3}\ \text{rad} \approx 0.06°$$

But this angle drops dramatically if we want to maintain coherence over larger targets. For example, for maintaining coherence over a 1 cm region, the max theta is:

$$\theta_\text{max} \approx 2 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{rad}$$

This means that for any given solid, the neutrino will only scatter coherently along the atoms that are approximately lined up behind it. Thus, we want very long detectors that are pointed towards the source for maximum efficiency.

## Appendix L: Superradiant Spin Transition Cross Section

We base our calculation on Equation 29 of [Arvinataki et al. (2024)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.04021). They compute that:

$$
     \Gamma_\text{reac}\sim \frac{1}{\text{3 hrs}}\left(\frac{n_s}{3\cdot10^{22}~\text{cm}^{-3}}\right)^2\left(\frac{R}{10~\text{cm}}\right)^4\left(\frac{100~\text{m}}{d}\right)^2,
$$

where:

* $\Gamma_\text{reac}$ is the rate of superradiance-enhanced spin-flip scattering events in the detector,
* $n_s$ is the number density of polarized spins in the detector medium,
* $R$ is the radius of the spherical polarized-spin detector volume,
* $d$ is the distance between the detector and the reactor source,

and they define the reactor source as emitting $10^{21}\, \frac{\bar{\nu}}{\text{sec}}$.

A VLCC has a beam of up to 60 meters. However, when we account for various supporting equipment, we probably want to only use a 40 meter diameter sphere for the actual fiducial volume. Thus, we'll set R to 20 meters.

We also note that our reactor source only emits at $10^{18}\, \frac{\bar{\nu}}{\text{sec}}$.

Keeping the spin density at the reference value of $3\cdot10^{22}~\text{cm}^{-3}$, the radius factor is $(R/10~\text{cm})^4 = 200^4 = 1.6\times10^{9}$, and the weaker source contributes a factor of $10^{18}/10^{21} = 10^{-3}$ (the rate is linear in flux, which is where the $1/d^2$ comes from).

Then, at 5 km:

$$
\Gamma_\text{reac} \sim \frac{1}{3~\text{hrs}} \times \left(1.6\times10^{9}\right) \times 10^{-3} \times \left(\frac{100~\text{m}}{5~\text{km}}\right)^2 = \frac{640}{3~\text{hrs}} \approx 213~\text{hr}^{-1}
$$

or about 5,100 events per day. At 50 km, the distance factor is 100 times smaller:

$$
\Gamma_\text{reac} \approx 2.1~\text{hr}^{-1}
$$

or about 51 events per day.

Note that since each sphere is mostly filled with a light gas, we are not weight constrained, and can fit many spheres on the tanker. An oil tanker can have a length of 340 meters, and assuming we give each sensing sphere 10 meters of padding (60 m unit size), we can fit 5 spheres, giving us:

$$
\Gamma_\text{reac} \approx 5 \times 213~\text{hr}^{-1} \approx 1{,}070~\text{hr}^{-1}
$$

or about 25,600 events per day at 5 km, and

$$
\Gamma_\text{reac} \approx 5 \times 2.1~\text{hr}^{-1} \approx 10.7~\text{hr}^{-1}
$$

or about 256 events per day at 50 km. At 100 km, the distance factor is 4 times smaller still:

$$
\Gamma_\text{reac} \approx 5 \times 0.53~\text{hr}^{-1} \approx 2.7~\text{hr}^{-1}
$$

or about 64 events per day.

## Appendix M: Bose-Einstein Condensate Superabsorption Cross Section

This idea is anticipated by [Jones & Formaggio (2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.11765), which proposes using a Bose-Einstein condensate of radioactive $^{83}$Rb as a superradiant neutrino emitter — and, run in reverse, as a superabsorber.

I differ from them in two ways: I use ordinary hydrogen instead of a radioactive isotope, and I target reactor antineutrinos instead of the cosmic neutrino background. The idea is to use a very large atomic-hydrogen Bose-Einstein condensate as a collectively enhanced absorber of reactor antineutrinos through inverse beta decay:

$$
\bar\nu_e + H \rightarrow n + e^+ + e^-_\text{spectator}
$$

An *ordinary* hydrogen BEC is nothing special here: it merely provides $N$ independent proton targets. The speculative step is to prepare a Jones-Formaggio-type correlated state, in which histories with different numbers of converted atoms interfere collectively. The states form a symmetric [Dicke ladder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dicke_state): with $m$ converted atoms and $N - m$ unconverted parents, the collective transition factor is

$$
G_m = (N - m)(m + 1)
$$

Initially ($m = 0$), the first capture proceeds at only the ordinary $N$-target rate. But near half conversion ($m \simeq N/2$), the factor peaks at

$$
G_\text{max} \simeq \frac{N^2}{4}
$$

giving the same $N^2$ scaling we saw with the spin transition detector — but for a *charged-current* reaction with real final-state particles.

This is a much stronger claim than ordinary coherent scattering. Recall from [Appendix K](#appendix-k-coherent-interactions) that coherence normally requires $|\vec q| R / \hbar \lesssim 1$: the positions of the scattering centers set relative phases, and for MeV neutrinos this restricts coherence to a vanishingly narrow forward cone. Jones and Formaggio's proposed loophole is that condensate atoms don't *have* separate localized positions — they all occupy the same spatial mode, so there is no positional record to dephase. Their distinctive claim is that the enhancement is stored in correlations within the decay medium itself, and thus survives even when the daughter particles (a neutron, a positron, and an electron — fermions, all escaping) leave the system. This goes substantially beyond conventional BEC physics and has not been derived explicitly for hydrogen IBD. The whole scheme hinges on the overlap between complete final states of different conversion histories being $\simeq 1$; if the escaping daughters carry away which-atom information, the cross terms vanish and the rate collapses back to ordinary $N$ scaling.

### Cross Section

If the hypothesis holds, the conditional cross section at rung $m$ of the ladder is

$$
\sigma_\text{BEC}(E, m) = (N - m)(m + 1)\, \sigma_\text{IBD}(E)
$$

which at the midpoint gives

$$
\sigma_\text{max}(E) \simeq \frac{N^2}{4}\, \sigma_\text{IBD}(E)
$$

The elementary IBD cross section is well understood ([Vogel & Beacom (1999)](https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-ph/9903554)). Rather than evaluating it at a single representative energy, we use the U-235 spectrum-averaged value from [Appendix C](#appendix-c-ibd-cross-section-of-proton), $\langle \sigma_\text{IBD} \rangle \approx 1.04 \times 10^{-43}$ cm² per proton, which already accounts for the 1.806 MeV threshold cutting off the low-energy bulk of the reactor spectrum. For a condensate of $N = 10^{19}$ hydrogen atoms (about 17 micrograms), the midpoint cross section is

$$
\sigma_\text{max} \simeq \frac{(10^{19})^2}{4} \times 1.04 \times 10^{-43}~\text{cm}^2 \approx 2.6 \times 10^{-6}~\text{cm}^2
$$

enormous by neutrino standards — nearly ninety times the CEvNS cross section of an entire supertanker, from micrograms of gas. Since the enhancement scales as $N^2$, each tenfold increase in atom count buys a hundredfold increase in peak cross section:

| Initial atoms $N$ | $\sigma_\text{max}$ (spectrum-averaged) |
| ----------------: | --------------------------: |
| $10^{16}$ | $2.6 \times 10^{-12}~\text{cm}^2$ |
| $10^{17}$ | $2.6 \times 10^{-10}~\text{cm}^2$ |
| $10^{18}$ | $2.6 \times 10^{-8}~\text{cm}^2$ |
| $10^{19}$ | $2.6 \times 10^{-6}~\text{cm}^2$ |

Without the hypothesized correlations, the same condensate at the midpoint is just $N/2$ independent protons:

$$
\sigma_\text{ordinary} = \frac{N}{2}\, \langle \sigma_\text{IBD} \rangle \approx 5.2 \times 10^{-25}~\text{cm}^2
$$

eighteen orders of magnitude smaller. The entire scheme lives or dies on that unverified factor of $N/2 = 5 \times 10^{18}$.

### Event Rates

Using the submarine source rate from [Appendix B](#appendix-b-number-of-antineutrinos-produced-by-a-nuclear-submarine), $R_{\bar\nu} \approx 1.44 \times 10^{18}\, \frac{\bar{\nu}}{\text{sec}}$, the flux at distance $d$ is $\Phi = R_{\bar\nu} / 4\pi d^2$, and the event rate for a $10^{19}$-atom condensate held at the midpoint is $\Gamma = \Phi \, \sigma_\text{max}$. At 5 km, $\Phi \approx 4.58 \times 10^{5}~\text{cm}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}$ (the same flux used in the main text's IBD and CEvNS estimates), so:

$$
\Gamma \approx 4.58 \times 10^{5}~\text{cm}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1} \times 2.6 \times 10^{-6}~\text{cm}^2 \approx 1.2~\text{s}^{-1} \approx 4{,}300~\text{hr}^{-1}
$$

or about 103,000 events per day. The rate falls off as $1/d^2$:

* **5 km:** $\approx$ 4,300 events/hour ($\approx$ 103,000 events/day)
* **50 km:** $\approx$ 43 events/hour ($\approx$ 1,030 events/day)
* **100 km:** $\approx$ 11 events/hour ($\approx$ 260 events/day)
* **200 km:** $\approx$ 2.7 events/hour ($\approx$ 64 events/day)

Because the underlying reaction is charged-current IBD, these rates are additionally subject to the $\bar\nu_e$ oscillation suppression discussed in the main text — unlike CEvNS, the 50-200 km figures above should be scaled down by the survival probability at those distances.

Remarkably, a single microgram-scale condensate would beat the five-sphere spin transition detector filling an entire supertanker by roughly a factor of three at equal source strength ([Appendix L](#appendix-l-superradiant-spin-transition-cross-section) rounds the source down to $10^{18}\, \bar\nu/\text{s}$, so its printed rates are ~1.44x conservative) — though note that each detection climbs the ladder by one rung, so the state slowly drifts away from the midpoint and would eventually need re-centering.

### Caveats

Even granting the physics, the engineering gap is severe. The largest hydrogen BEC ever demonstrated ([Fried et al. (1998)](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/9809017)) contained about $10^9$ atoms — a factor of $10^{10}$ below the $10^{19}$ target. And the correlated state cannot be prepared for free: hydrogen is stable, so reaching the midpoint of the ladder requires actually converting $N/2 = 5 \times 10^{18}$ atoms via an intense preparation source of electron antineutrinos, and the microkelvin condensate must survive all $5 \times 10^{18}$ of those nuclear reactions without heating out of existence. One point in hydrogen's favor: unlike radioactive-condensate proposals, a hydrogen ladder halts the moment the preparation source is switched off, so the state can in principle be parked at the sensitive midpoint indefinitely.
